By the time the bus reached the depot, the storm had already chewed through
the valley and moved on, leaving the road washed and silver and the whole
town smelling of wet limestone.

Owen got off with his bag and stood there long enough to feel like a person
instead of a shoulder and a backpack. Nobody met him. He had not really
expected anyone to, but standing there with a plan for nobody was its own
small ceremony, and he gave it a moment before walking into town.

The library had a clock above the door with numbers that did not match the
time inside, and inside, the librarian had already pulled the map he
photocopied twice a week, as if the map itself were on payroll. Owen nodded.
The librarian nodded back. Between the two of them, that was a conversation.

He sat in the reading room and spread the pages out flat, and for a while he
did nothing, because doing nothing next to the pages was how he let his
attention arrive on its own. The valley had two roads, really, one for
summer and one for the rest of the year, and the flood had made a liar of
the summer road in the same week his meeting was supposed to happen.

There were rules for days like this, though nobody had ever written them
down. Rule one: do not drive a road that is rated by rumor. Rule two: when
the town is small, the news is fast, so ask at the diner and not the
internet. Rule three, the old one he invented himself at every job: be
useful to somebody today, even if the somebody is only yourself tomorrow.

He copied the shorter route by hand onto his notebook, the way pencil makes
a road into a personal fact instead of a rumor. He underlined the bridge
milestone twice. He added a note about how this week had already cost him a
day, and how the day was not recovered by arguing about it at a counter.

The diner had booths and a radio playing to nobody in particular, and the
waitress asked him if he wanted the soup like it was a personal question.
He did want the soup. He ordered two. One for now, he said, and one for the
road, which is the sort of sentence that buys a traveler more patience in a
small town than any business plan will.

After lunch he paid, tipped, thanked the cook by name, and went back out
into the washed bright afternoon, where the storm had helpfully knocked the
whole schedule down a peg and left everything else standing.
