The morning light came through the kitchen window in a wide bar, the way it
always did on the first warm week of spring, and Marta stood in it with her
coffee going cold in her hands.

She had stayed up too late again, not for any good reason, only because the
house was quiet and the quiet was finally hers. The dog slept under the table
where the sun would find him in about an hour, and the kids' shoes sat in a
line by the door, two pairs, different sizes, both left in a hurry.

Her list for the day was short and honest: one phone call she did not want to
make, one load of laundry, one walk. She wrote the walk down even though she
knew it would happen, because there was a pleasure in writing down the things
you were already going to do. It made the day look fuller than it was.

Outside, the street was still asleep. A single truck rolled by, throwing its
shadow along the fences, and then the quiet came back, the deep kind you only
get in a neighborhood right before other people's days start up. She took a
long drink and finally tasted the coffee, which had gone lukewarm and slightly
bitter, and decided that this was fine. Some mornings you drink it hot; some
mornings you just drink it.

Her mother used to say that patience was mostly just noticing things twice.
Marta thought about that now, watching the sun bar creep across the floor,
first touching the dog's paws, then his shoulder, then nothing at all, since
the dog had wandered off to follow the kitchen smells. Maybe her mother had
meant something different. Maybe she had meant that the second look is the
one that pays, and the first is just the down payment.

The phone call would not take more than ten minutes, she knew that, and she
knew it was the kind of thing that grows teeth the longer you look at it. So
she set the mug down, rinsed it, stood it in the rack, and picked up the
phone before the quiet could talk her out of it.

The number rang four times. A machine picked up, calm and bored, and Marta
left a clear, short message with her name said twice, once slowly, like a
person who expects to be remembered. She hung up and felt the small click of
it being done, which was better than any reply.

Then she opened the back door and let the cold morning air push her out of
the house, and the dog raced ahead down the steps, ears up, ready to pretend
they both did this every day.
